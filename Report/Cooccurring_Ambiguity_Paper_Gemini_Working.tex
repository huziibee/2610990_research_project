\begin{abstract}
A single instruction to a robot may contain several ambiguities at once: an indirect request, an unclear object reference, an imprecise time, or a missing destination. These ambiguities need not prevent action if the surrounding scene and conversation provide enough information to resolve them. This paper plans a text-to-text study using Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite to recover an intended goal, ground a structured task in input evidence, and request clarification when a critical detail is unresolved. Direct generation and structured interpretation are the principal comparison; independent repeated-model review remains optional. The current evaluation-only release contains 100 cases with author-approved routes and strategy sequences. It does not yet provide complete semantic task references or simulated clarification replies. Primary task-correctness and multi-turn experiments therefore remain pending reference completion and protocol freeze. No numerical performance claims are made.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
People rarely describe everyday tasks as complete lists of robot actions. A person might say, ``Put the other cup beside the plate on the left, then move it over there.'' The robot must determine which cup and plate are meant, what ``left'' refers to, whether ``it'' denotes the cup or plate, and where the final destination is. Some answers may be present in the scene or earlier conversation; others may require a question. The useful outcome is not a fluent paraphrase but a task description with the right actions, objects, and constraints.

We call a command \emph{co-occurringly ambiguous} when it contains at least two potentially uncertain aspects, such as its intended action, object, location, time, or scope. Co-occurrence does not necessarily mean that the ambiguities depend on each other. For example, identifying a tray may also identify its assigned recipient, whereas choosing a time and choosing a shelf may be independent decisions. The number of ambiguous expressions alone therefore cannot tell us whether the robot should ask for help.

Prior work has studied indirect requests in task-oriented dialogue \cite{mannekote}, classification and clarification of robot commands \cite{clara}, clarification requests during collaborative tasks \cite{codraw}, and mapping vague commands to structured control calls \cite{clarifyvc}. These studies provide important methods, but they do not by themselves establish which combination is necessary to turn instructions with several ambiguity types into fully grounded task specifications. Work on integrating language models into robotic architectures also cautions against evaluating language interpretation only as an isolated fluent output \cite{sarathy}.

Our scope is deliberately limited to \emph{text-to-text interpretation}. The input may describe a physical environment and the robot's available actions, but this study does not perform motion planning or test physical execution. We define an \emph{action-ready task} as a structured description whose required actions, arguments, and constraints are determined from the supplied information or an explicit user clarification. Action readiness does not certify that a real robot can safely execute the task.

This paper sets out three contributions to be evaluated: (i) a goal-first, evidence-grounded task representation that distinguishes resolved details from unresolved ones; (ii) a controlled study of whether this structure and its contextual checks improve over direct Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite generation; and (iii) an analysis of whether a second Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite interpretation and optional adjudication justify their additional cost. These are research aims, not claims of demonstrated improvements.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\subsection{Indirect meaning and task interpretation}
A request can communicate an intended action without naming that action directly. Searle distinguishes the literal form of an utterance from an indirect act performed through it \cite{searle1975}, while Grice explains how conversational context supports inferences beyond the words explicitly spoken \cite{grice1975}. These accounts motivate interpreting communicative intent, but do not supply an algorithm for generating action-ready robot tasks. Mannekote \emph{et al.} study the synthetic generation of indirect requests to make task-oriented dialogue data more natural \cite{mannekote}. Zhang \emph{et al.} examine indirect speech in physical human--robot collaboration, where the literal wording and intended collaborative response may differ \cite{tool}. These works establish why interpreting only the surface wording is inadequate. They do not, however, provide a general test of multiple interacting ambiguities within one task specification.

\subsection{Ambiguity and contextual grounding}
AmbiK provides ambiguous task instructions situated in kitchen environments, illustrating how the same instruction can require additional knowledge or clarification \cite{ambik}. VAGUE investigates how visual context can clarify ambiguous expressions \cite{vague}. Context can make one interpretation more plausible than another, but a plausible interpretation still needs evidence.

A useful distinction is between \emph{co-occurrence} and \emph{dependence}. Two ambiguous fields co-occur when both appear in an instruction; they depend on one another when choosing a value for one constrains the possible value of the other. For example, selecting a labelled medicine tray may determine its recipient. In contrast, the tray and an unrelated delivery deadline may each be resolved independently. This distinction affects how ambiguity is evaluated, even when both instructions contain the same number of ambiguity labels.

\subsection{Clarification in human--robot interaction}
CLARA distinguishes ambiguity that can be resolved through interaction from commands outside the robot's feasible action repertoire, using scene-aware uncertainty and feasibility reasoning \cite{clara}. We adapt only this \emph{diagnostic distinction}, not CLARA's sampling-based uncertainty estimator, feasibility classifier, or additional terminal state. Madureira and Schlangen analyse clarification requests in collaborative CoDraw dialogues, showing that clarification can concern specific missing or uncertain task information \cite{codraw}. An important distinction is that uncertainty about a model prediction does not necessarily mean a required task detail is missing. For the present text-to-text problem, clarification must ultimately be judged by whether it resolves an execution-critical field.

\subsection{Structured task generation and robot capabilities}
ClarifyVC studies the translation of ambiguous vehicle-control commands into structured, schema-aligned calls and uses ambiguity-oriented data augmentation \cite{clarifyvc}. This makes it a close methodological reference, although vehicle functions are more constrained than many general robot tasks. The selected hosted model is Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite, requested as \texttt{gemini-2.5-flash-lite}; Google documents its thinking support and API configuration \cite{geminimodel,geminithinking}. Direct generation must receive a documented reasoning budget comparable to the structured procedure. Additional prompts alone do not establish a new reasoning capability; live configuration compatibility remains to be verified before evaluation.

\subsection{Literature-derived evaluation perspectives}
ClarifyVC motivates a three-tier diagnostic separation: single-turn parsing, conservative clarification, and grounding across multiple turns \cite{clarifyvc}. We adapt this \emph{evaluation organization}, not the vehicle-control algorithms, training data, or safety assurances. Sarathy \emph{et al.} motivate distinguishing the interpretation of the speaker's intent, action selection, referential binding, and relationships between arguments \cite{sarathy}. These evaluator categories are not claims that our generator implements Sarathy's cognitive architecture. AmbiK's human-preferences, common-sense-knowledge and safety distinctions motivate per-type help analysis rather than a single blanket preference for asking questions \cite{ambik}. The spontaneous instruction clarification requests in CoDraw-iCR motivate coding \emph{what information} a question seeks \cite{codraw}. IndirectRequests supplies distinct linguistic review dimensions---appropriateness, unambiguity, and world understanding---that should not be collapsed into a single intent score \cite{mannekote}. VAGUE demonstrates the importance of visual scene cues for disambiguation \cite{vague}; our controlled removal of a \emph{textual scene description} is only a text-only methodological adaptation, never a reproduced visual benchmark or image-understanding experiment.

Grice's cooperative principles of quantity, quality, relation, and manner and Searle's distinction between literal utterances and indirect acts provide theoretical motivation for why concise, relevant questions and evidence-supported interpretation matter \cite{grice1975,searle1975}. Zhang \emph{et al.} add empirical motivation from indirect speech in physical collaboration \cite{tool}. These are interpretive motivations, not implemented pragmatic inference rules or empirical evaluations of human trust in our text-only system.

\subsection{Research gap}
The literature provides complementary accounts of indirect meaning, grounding, clarification, and structured outputs. The open question addressed here is narrower and testable: for commands containing several ambiguities, does explicitly representing a task's goal, grounded arguments, and unresolved details improve the correctness of the final task compared with direct generation? A related question is whether repeated reasoning with the same model adds value beyond the structured approach. These questions require reference task specifications, not only labels such as ``execute'' and ``clarify.''

\section{Research Questions and Hypotheses}
\textbf{RQ1 (structured interpretation):} Does an explicit, evidence-grounded task representation with action-readiness validation improve complete-task correctness over direct generation with the same hosted model version, input context, and comparable inference budgets?

\emph{H1:} Structured interpretation will increase the proportion of fully correct task specifications and reduce unsupported argument choices relative to direct generation.

\textbf{RQ2 (context and ambiguity dependence):} How do scene descriptions, dialogue history, and explicit checks between task arguments affect correct interpretation, particularly when ambiguities depend on each other?

\emph{H2:} Removing relevant context will reduce grounding accuracy; checking dependencies will be most useful for examples with linked arguments, rather than all multi-ambiguity examples equally.

\textbf{RQ3 (repeated reasoning):} Does an additional independent Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite interpretation, with optional Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite adjudication, improve correctness enough to justify extra inference cost?

\emph{H3:} Review may correct some initial interpretations, but its net benefit will be smaller when compared with a structured system given a comparable inference budget. This hypothesis may be rejected if review adds no measurable value.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Task and output definition}
The system receives a human instruction $u$, a scene description $s$, previous dialogue $d$, and a natural-language capability description $c$. The model-visible capability description is natural language, not an automatically trusted machine-readable catalog. An optional human-reviewed catalog, bound by a digest of that visible description, supports deterministic membership and required-argument checks; catalog provenance and contextual support still require independent verification. Its input is
\begin{equation}
 x=(u,s,d,c),\qquad f(x)\in\{T,q\},
 \label{eq:task}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is a structured task and $q$ is a clarification question. The task $T$ contains the intended goal, ordered actions, their arguments, constraints, and evidence for the chosen values. Missing values remain explicitly unresolved rather than being guessed. For the set $R(T)$ of required arguments, we define
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{Ready}(T)=
 \left[\bigwedge_{a\in R(T)}\operatorname{Grounded}(a,x)\right]
 \land\operatorname{Consistent}(T,c).
 \label{eq:ready}
\end{equation}
Here, $\operatorname{Grounded}(a,x)$ is a \emph{semantic evaluation criterion}: a reviewer must establish that the value follows from the instruction, scene, dialogue, or a released clarification. $\operatorname{Consistent}(T,c)$ requires compatible actions, arguments, and dependencies, and capability support when a verified catalog is available. The current implementation checks the fixed JSON shape, permissible evidence pointers, selected dependency consistency rules, and catalog identifier/argument membership. It does \emph{not} prove that an evidence span entails a chosen referent, that a described capability is physically available, or that an action achieves the intended goal. The only model-output readiness values remain \texttt{ACTION\_READY} and \texttt{CLARIFY}; diagnostics do not create a third route.

\subsection{Proposed interpretation pipeline}
Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the implemented generation scaffold and proposed evaluation boundary. S1 first recovers a goal hypothesis, then generates a grounded task; a model-generated goal is not an evidence source. Deterministic validation checks structure, exact evidence references and internal relationships; capability semantics and full correctness require independent scoring. Optional S2 generates two interpretations from the original input independently; the second branch never sees the first. Only subsequent adjudication may see both proposals, and must decide using input evidence. A clarification answer returns to grounding and revalidation. At most five simulated turns are allowed, and an unavailable answer or unresolved turn cap remains unresolved. The initial implementation is a development scaffold, not an evaluated or semantically verified system. Separately, an evaluator-only diagnostic layer projects generated tasks into relationship graphs and audits capabilities and clarification continuity; these reports do not feed back into prompts or change either terminal readiness value.

\subsection{Sarathy- and CLARA-inspired operational diagnostics}
We retain the generator's existing task structure rather than attempt to reproduce Sarathy \emph{et al.}'s DIARC information structure \cite{sarathy}. The task contains an intended goal, ordered capability-labelled actions, typed arguments with model-visible evidence pointers, constraints, unresolved fields, and explicit dependencies. An \emph{evaluator-only projection} maps the goal and action sequence to a CPC-inspired graph with proposed goal-to-action realization links. It separately maps typed object and location arguments to an SPC-inspired referent layer. When a reviewer supplies canonical entity identifiers, the layer can check whether two action arguments refer to the same entity; without those identifiers, typed surface values are only provisional labels, not anaphora resolution. A comparison with an independently approved reference graph identifies missing or extra action structures, referent differences, and dependency edges, optionally using an explicitly supplied action alignment. It also detects duplicate relations, conflicting referent labels, and cycles in normalized precedence relations: $a$ \texttt{before} $b$ means $a$ precedes $b$, whereas $a$ \texttt{depends\_on} $b$ makes $b$ a prerequisite of $a$. These mechanical differences guide subsequent human review; literal goal equality, graph coverage, or a valid pointer is not a semantic-correctness judgment. No automatic graph-isomorphism solver or Sarathy-style semantic parser is claimed.

Motivated by CLARA's separation of ambiguous and infeasible commands \cite{clara}, a second evaluator-only report distinguishes: (i) execution-critical information still unresolved in the task, (ii) values claimed to be resolved by a quoted passage in the visible instruction, scene, or dialogue, and (iii) capability or required-argument limitations relative to a separately reviewed catalog. The catalog is bound to the original visible capability description by its SHA-256 digest, and its quoted evidence and distinct preparer/verifier declarations are checked. These declarations are not identity authentication, and a matching quotation is not proof of capability semantics. Consequently, absent catalogs yield \emph{unknown capability support}, not inferred infeasibility; an unsupported action never silently becomes a third generator route. A field claimed both resolved and critical is flagged as inconsistent rather than automatically repaired.

Finally, a post-hoc clarification-continuity audit replays only approved answer turns into a copy of the prior dialogue and revalidates the task at each recorded turn (at most five replies). It checks that each question corresponds to the preceding unresolved field, that the recorded answer matches the approved release, and that previously established action/argument bindings are retained. Reopened fields, omitted arguments, or contradictory slot updates are reported unless a correction was explicitly authorized for that particular turn and slot. This is a CLARA-inspired revalidation diagnostic, \emph{not} CLARA's uncertainty estimator or an assertion that a syntactically matched answer pragmatically resolved the ambiguity. Both the exact question-to-answer release gate and independent complete-task semantic judging remain separate.

\subsection{Ablations and evaluation}
Within S1, planned ablations independently remove goal-first interpretation, structured intermediate grounding, scene context, dependency checks, and dialogue history. Dialogue eligibility must distinguish merely present history from history relevant to the interpretation. Dependency analysis requires a frozen eligibility annotation identifying linked fields; ambiguity-type labels alone do not establish dependence. Eligible counts are not yet established. The linked-dependency analysis will remain exploratory.

The primary measure is \emph{complete task correctness}: whether all required actions, arguments, and constraints match an independently adjudicated reference in meaning. Secondary measures include per-field grounding accuracy, unsupported-argument rate, missing-critical-field rate, execute/clarify decisions, unnecessary clarification, clarification turns, and inference cost per correctly resolved task. Equivalent task descriptions are judged semantically rather than by literal JSON string matching. Each system is evaluated over five prespecified repeated runs, with identical cases and documented decoding settings; case-level paired comparisons and uncertainty intervals account for variation across cases and runs. Runs are repeated for the ablations where computationally feasible, and any reduced repetition is reported explicitly. Confidence intervals are not calculated by treating repeated outputs from the same case as independent examples. Any evaluation using simulated answers will be clearly separated from evaluation with real user replies.

\subsection{Literature-grounded diagnostic protocol (evaluator only)}
The predeclared primary endpoint remains \emph{complete task correctness} for S0 versus S1 on the same 100 protected cases and five seeds; secondary literature diagnostics cannot redefine it. A valid task must have the correct intended goal, complete ordered action sequence, correct arguments and constraints, correct dependencies, source-supported evidence, and no unsupported detail. The existing evaluator scores independently verified field judgments and cumulative trajectory success, averaging answer branches within each case/run and then repeated runs within cases for paired inference. Operational errors are denominator failures, not fabricated human field judgments. Route agreement and JSON/schema validity do not establish semantic correctness. The resulting S1--S0 difference uses paired case resampling, not an independent 500-prediction sample.

\textbf{Tier 1 (single-turn interpretation):} independently review intent correctness, action-name correctness, argument correctness, required-parameter completeness and whether unsupported assumptions were introduced. Each binary accuracy is the count of reviewed correct outputs divided by its eligible, successfully predicted review count. Unsupported-assumption \emph{incidence} is the fraction with any unsupported assumption; its complementary absence rate is displayed separately. Eligibility and denominators are mandatory; missing reviews block publication rather than being treated as negative labels.

\textbf{Tier 2 (clarification):} against human-approved critical unknown fields, record the set detected by the prediction, whether a question was asked, its relevance, and the information sought (referent, attribute, location, relation, action, quantity, time, scope, confirmation, or other). With $N$ denoting a reference that needs clarification and $A$ an observed question, clarification precision is $\sum(N\land A)/\sum A$, recall is $\sum(N\land A)/\sum N$, and unnecessary clarification is $\sum(\neg N\land A)/\sum\neg N$. Critical-field recall is $|G\cap P|/|G|$ aggregated over eligible outputs, where $G$ and $P$ are approved and detected critical-field sets. Question relevance uses \emph{actually asked} questions only. Empty denominators are unreported, not treated as zero. Original exact approved question-variant matching remains a separate frozen answer-availability gate, not a learned semantic equivalence judge.

\textbf{Tier 3 (multi-turn grounding):} after each real or explicitly simulated clarification answer, review whether dialogue remains consistent, previously established parameters are preserved, and the produced task is correct. These transition judgments require an eligible observed turn greater than zero. Cumulative correctness through turns $t=0,\ldots,5$ is taken only from the existing complete-trajectory scorer: a branch succeeds by $t$ if it has produced a fully correct action-ready task by that turn. Branch results are averaged within case/run, then across runs/cases. A capped or unmatched simulated reply is not a successful completion.

\textbf{Sarathy-derived evaluator fields:} intent and action-name accuracy reuse Tier 1; referential-binding correctness and relational correctness are separately reviewed only where the frozen reference defines an applicable referent or explicit relation. A relation is correct only if its endpoints, type, direction and task semantics are correct; merely valid JSON pointers are insufficient. Existing dependency consistency is a limited syntactic/structural check, not an oracle of real relational truth. A separate implemented structural projection produces proposed CPC goal--action links, typed SPC-like referents, and dependency difference diagnostics; it neither establishes referential truth nor changes this tier's human semantic ratings.

\textbf{Other grounded secondary analyses:} AmbiK-type strata report appropriate-help accuracy with separate denominators for human preferences, commonsense knowledge, and safety (the category must be independently annotated for applicability). CoDraw-iCR motivates descriptive counts of question information targets; our coding taxonomy is a project adaptation rather than the original CoDraw label inventory. IndirectRequests motivates independent binary reviews for appropriateness, unambiguity, and world understanding on eligible indirect commands. For a VAGUE-inspired text-only contrast, compare independently reviewed intent accuracy between identical case/seed/branch/turn configurations with the full versus withheld \emph{textual} scene description. Calculate the within-pair full-minus-withheld difference, average repetitions/branches within case, and report the number of independent cases. Neither visual understanding nor causal scene effects are claimed without matched runs and review.

A separately authenticated release is still required: reviewer names or hashes alone are not proof of human annotation. \subsection{Failure analysis and scoring procedure}
Every incorrect prediction will be assigned a primary failure category using the same adjudicated reference: wrong intended goal, wrong entity or argument, missing constraint, unsupported inferred detail, unnecessary clarification, or failure to clarify a critical unknown. Secondary tags may be retained when more than one error occurs, but each case contributes once to the primary error count. Reviewers will examine the input evidence and generated structured task, not only the model's explanation. Table~\ref{tab:failure} reserves space for this breakdown in Results.

To score complete task correctness, an evaluator checks the meaning of the full task, including action order and constraints; schema validity alone is insufficient. For a clarification-required case, the initial question is evaluated separately from any task produced after a supplied answer. Disagreements in semantic scoring are resolved using the documented annotation protocol. Results will be reported with the number of eligible cases for each metric.
